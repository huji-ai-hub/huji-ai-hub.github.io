For more than six decades, a fundamental question hovered over neuroscience: how does the brain process the image falling on the retina and extract its meaning?

A new study published in the journal *Science*, led by Prof. Arthur Konnerth of the Technical University of Munich and co-authored by Prof. Israel Nelken of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, has finally provided decisive evidence for a theory first proposed in 1962.

The research focuses on oriented lines, such as the edges of a door, tree branches, or the horizon line, which are key features of any visual scene. The brain is known to be especially sensitive to these orientation cues, and the study clarifies the neural mechanisms behind this sensitivity.

The findings advance our understanding of how biological visual systems work, with potential implications for computational models of vision and artificial neural networks.
